THE DETECTION GUIDE

How long does alcohol stay in your system?

There is no single alcohol-detection window. Tests may measure ethanol itself or markers of prior exposure, and those results do not all describe current intoxication.

Quick Facts Key Takeaways at a Glance
  • Clearance vs Detection: Blood alcohol concentration (BAC) clears at roughly 0.015% per hour (within 24 hours), but metabolites remain detectable in urine (EtG: up to 3 days) and hair (up to 90 days).
  • Impairment vs Biomarkers: Feeling sober does not mean all alcohol metabolites have cleared; conversely, detectable hair or urine biomarkers do not prove current impairment.
  • Metabolic Capacity: The human liver metabolizes roughly 1 standard drink (14 g pure ethanol) per 1 to 2 hours at an enzymatically fixed rate.
  • Testing Methods: Blood and breath measure immediate pharmacologically active ethanol; urine and hair test historical consumption.

Three questions that are often mixed together

“Is alcohol still in my system?” may mean whether ethanol is currently circulating, whether a test can identify earlier drinking, or whether a person is still impaired. These are related questions, but they need different evidence. A simple time-since-last-drink calculation cannot answer all three.

A blood alcohol estimate concerns a modeled concentration at a point in time. A biomarker test may look for a product or consequence of alcohol exposure. Feeling recovered is a subjective assessment. Keep those categories separate when reading test information or interpreting a calculator result.

Ethanol clearance versus biomarker persistence

The body processes ethanol into other substances. Some laboratory tests use markers connected with that process to provide evidence of exposure beyond the time that ethanol itself is detectable. A positive marker does not automatically reveal an exact drink count or the person's state at the time of collection.

Similarly, a negative result has to be understood in relation to the test's detection capability, cutoff and sampling time. It is not a general certificate that no drinking occurred. Clinical interpretation belongs with the ordering professional or laboratory, especially if the result has treatment or legal consequences.

Why detection windows vary

Amount and pattern of use, the sample type, the analytical method and the reporting threshold influence detection. A single episode and repeated exposure can produce different marker patterns. Health factors may also matter, depending on the analyte and clinical setting.

Online tables often compress these variables into one number. That can make broad educational guidance look like an individual prediction. Do not use a maximum published window as a personal guarantee, and do not use a short published window to assume a test must be negative. Ask the laboratory what its result actually supports.

A closer look at urine tests

Urine ethanol and urine EtG/EtS are not the same test. Ethanol testing looks for the alcohol itself, whereas EtG and EtS are metabolites used to assess exposure. The distinction changes how the result should be discussed and why a general “urine alcohol” deadline is unreliable.

Our urine alcohol guide explains the difference in more detail. If a test has been ordered, obtain the name, cutoff and instructions from the provider. Do not alter hydration or use supposed cleansing products to try to influence the result; this site does not offer test-evasion advice.

Blood, breath and the timing of impairment

Blood and breath tests can provide more direct information about alcohol concentration than a drink-count formula. Yet concentration is only part of a broader assessment. Tolerance does not make an elevated concentration harmless, and fatigue or another substance can impair a person even with little or no alcohol present.

Odor is not a measurement, either. A person without noticeable alcohol breath may still have circulating alcohol. Read the breath alcohol explanation for the distinction between smell, sampling and an instrument reading.

Where an online estimate fits

The BAC estimator helps demonstrate how ethanol quantity, body-water assumptions and time affect a simplified model. It does not predict urine biomarkers, hair analysis, PEth or the result of a formal testing program. It also does not assess whether a person is medically stable.

Use it for learning, and use the appropriate professional pathway for clinical testing or urgent symptoms. For transport decisions, avoid driving after drinking rather than relying on an estimate. The most useful next step is often to clarify which question you are actually trying to answer.

Saliva, hair and other less-discussed samples

Oral-fluid (saliva) tests generally look for ethanol itself and are used in some roadside and workplace settings because sampling is simple and observed. Because saliva concentration tracks recent circulating alcohol reasonably closely, the practical detection story resembles blood and breath more than urine biomarkers: relevant while ethanol is present, and increasingly insensitive afterwards.

Hair testing works differently. Alcohol markers measured in hair segments — such as fatty-acid ethyl esters and ethyl glucuronide — are used mainly in clinical and forensic contexts to examine drinking patterns over weeks or months, not to answer whether someone is intoxicated today. A hair result can be affected by hair treatments and hygiene, and interpretation is specialized laboratory work. It cannot tell you anything about a single evening, which is exactly the mismatch that makes generic “alcohol stays in your system for X days” claims so misleading.

Sweat-patch and transdermal monitoring exist too, mostly in monitoring programs. Each method answers its own question, has its own cutoff and requires its own expertise to interpret. If you are facing any of these tests, the ordering provider — not a website — is the right source for what the result will mean.

Workplace, clinical and monitoring contexts

Alcohol testing appears in three common settings, and their purposes differ. Workplace programs usually screen for present impairment, so they lean on breath or saliva ethanol with confirmatory testing. Clinical care may measure ethanol to guide treatment or assess a patient, and clinical laboratories also handle the biomarker panels used to examine longer-term drinking. Monitoring programs — such as those linked to legal or treatment requirements — often rely on the longer-memory markers precisely because they detect drinking that ethanol tests would miss.

This is why “how long does alcohol stay in your system?” cannot be answered with one number: an ethanol test and a biomarker panel are looking for different things on purpose. A negative on one and a positive on the other is not a contradiction; it is the two tests doing their respective jobs. If someone tells you a single hour figure for “system clearance,” they are describing only one test at one threshold.

Questions people ask

Does a positive EtG test mean someone is currently drunk?

No. EtG is a marker of exposure and is not a direct measure of present impairment. The laboratory result needs context and test-specific interpretation.

Can a BAC calculator predict a urine test?

No. A BAC formula does not model the metabolism, thresholds and sample factors used by urine biomarker testing.

Can water clear alcohol from the system faster?

Water does not provide a reliable shortcut to ethanol clearance. Changing hydration can also affect urine sample characteristics and should not be used to manipulate testing.

Why do different websites list different hours?

They may refer to different analytes, doses, methods or cutoffs, or oversimplify the evidence. Look for the exact test and the source rather than comparing isolated time ranges.

Can saliva or hair tests show whether I am drunk right now?

Saliva ethanol tests track recent alcohol and are used for present-impairment screening, but hair testing examines markers that reflect drinking patterns over weeks. A hair result cannot show current intoxication from a single evening.

Why would a urine test be positive after breath and blood are negative?

Ethanol may already be cleared while exposure markers such as EtG and EtS remain detectable. The tests are designed to answer different questions: present concentration versus evidence of prior drinking.

Does exercise remove alcohol faster?

No. The vast majority of ethanol is processed by the liver at a rate that exercise does not meaningfully change. Sweating may alter hydration and how you feel, but it does not accelerate elimination.

What is PEth and how is it different from a breath test?

PEth is a blood biomarker formed in cell membranes in the presence of alcohol. It reflects drinking patterns over roughly the preceding weeks and is used in clinical and forensic assessment, unlike a breath test, which measures current alcohol concentration.
