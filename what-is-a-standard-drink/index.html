STANDARD DRINKS

One glass is not one drink.

A "standard drink" is a fixed amount of pure alcohol — 14 g in the US, 10 g in Australia, 8 g in the UK. Your glass holds whatever was poured. See the difference, country by country.

The problem with "I had two drinks"

Nobody counts ethanol. People count glasses, bottles, rounds and hours. But blood alcohol tracks grams of alcohol, and a glass can hold anywhere from 9 g to 32 g depending on what was poured into it.

A standard drink is a fixed amount of pure alcohol that a country uses for education, labels and guidance. It is an accounting unit — not a safe serving, and not what a bar necessarily serves. Once you can translate your actual glass into standard drinks, every calculator and guideline on this site starts making sense.

Compare real servings

The differences look small until you pour. An Australian "standard drink" label on a bottle of wine (typically 7.7 per bottle) and a US count of the same bottle (5.5) describe identical ethanol. Any guide that mixes definitions without saying which country it is using is quietly wrong by up to 40%.

Where real servings go wrong

Three systematic traps push real servings above their "standard" versions. First, strength creep: average craft beer sits at 6–8% ABV, and 8% hard seltzers and 13–15% wines have normalized labels that hold 1.3–2.3 standard drinks each. Second, pour drift: home and restaurant wine glasses commonly take 250 ml, not 150. Third, default doubles: many bars pour 2 shots in a cocktail as standard, doubling the ethanol before the first sip.

The result is predictable: someone "having two drinks" can be at six standard drinks without miscounting anything. The 0.08 BAC page shows exactly how few standard drinks push a typical person over the legal limit — the answer is fewer than most people guess.

Reading labels like a pro

US spirits carry ABV but not standard-drink counts; Australian labels must state standard drinks per container; UK labels state units. Wherever you are, the universal move is the same: volume in ml × ABV% ÷ 100 × 0.789 = grams of ethanol. Divide by 14 for US drinks, 10 for Australian, 13.45 for Canadian, or divide ml×ABV÷100 by 10 for UK units.

The alcohol volume calculator does this arithmetic for any serving, and the BAC calculator takes it further into estimated concentration. Both are educational models — precise arithmetic on imprecise recollection of what was actually poured.

Why this matters for safety

Standard-drink literacy is not about drinking less by rule. It is about the fact that every downstream decision — how impaired you are, when you can drive, whether a guideline applies — depends on the grams, not the glassware. People who underestimate their servings by half also underestimate their BAC by half, and then make a driving decision on the wrong number.

If you took one lesson from this page: when in doubt, treat every poured drink as bigger than "standard" — because in most modern serving sizes, it is. See the disclaimer for the limits of all estimates on this site.

Questions people ask

What counts as one standard drink in the US?

About 14 grams of pure alcohol: a 355 ml beer at 5% ABV, a 150 ml glass of wine at 12%, or a 45 ml shot of 40% spirits. Any serving with the same ethanol total counts the same, whatever the glass looks like.

Is a UK unit the same as a US drink?

No. A UK unit is 10 ml (about 8 g) of pure alcohol, so one US standard drink of 14 g is about 1.75 UK units. A UK "single" (25 ml at 40%) is one unit; the US shot is larger.

Why is an Australian standard drink smaller?

Australia defines a standard drink as 10 g of ethanol, close to the UK unit. Canada uses 13.45 g. Health guidance, pour sizes and label rules differ accordingly — the ethanol math does not.

Do bartenders pour standard drinks?

Often not. Restaurant wine glasses of 250 ml, doubles served as default, and craft beer at 6–9% ABV all mean one ordered "drink" can be 1.5–2.5 standard drinks. Labels and measured pours are the reliable reference.

How many standard drinks are in a bottle of wine?

A 750 ml bottle at 13% ABV contains about 76.9 g of ethanol — roughly 5.5 US standard drinks, 7.7 UK units, 7.7 Australian standard drinks or 5.7 Canadian drinks.
