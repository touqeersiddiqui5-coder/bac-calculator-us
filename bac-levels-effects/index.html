BAC LEVELS & EFFECTS

What each BAC level actually does.

From 0.00% to the lethal range: typical effects, legal thresholds and the danger signs — on an interactive chart you can slide through, not a drinking guide.

How to read this levels chart

Blood alcohol concentration is measured in grams of alcohol per 100 milliliters of blood, written as a percentage. The ranges below describe typical, population-level effects reported in educational and public-health literature. They are not a menu of sensations to aim for, and they are not a description of any specific person at any specific number.

Two people with identical BAC values can feel and function quite differently. Body chemistry, tolerance, food, fatigue, medication and pace of drinking all change the real experience. The only use of this chart that we endorse is understanding how quickly effects escalate — and how far below "feeling drunk" measurable impairment begins.

Why the ranges get steep so fast

Alcohol's effects on the brain are not linear. Early changes affect the frontal areas involved in judgment and self-assessment, which is why the first measurable effect is often not noticing impairment. As concentration rises, balance, speech, temperature regulation and finally breathing reflexes degrade in sequence.

Because the liver clears roughly 0.015 percentage points per hour on average, moving from 0.16% to 0.08% takes about five hours — and you are impaired at both numbers. The sobering-up guide explains why only time lowers BAC.

Levels and the law

US law sets 0.08% as the per se illegal limit for ordinary adult drivers nationwide, with 0.04% for commercial (CDL) drivers and zero tolerance (usually 0.01–0.02%) for drivers under 21. Utah uses 0.05%. Canada and Australia commonly use 0.05%, with warning ranges and novice zero rules layered below. See the legal-limit guide for how enforcement actually works, including refusal and impairment-based charges.

Per se limits mean a test above the number is itself the offense — no proof of visible bad driving needed. But the reverse is not true: below the limit does not mean legal to drive, because impairment and "driving under the influence" charges can apply at any measurable level.

Reading the danger zone honestly

Alcohol poisoning is not a rare edge case. It typically follows rapid consumption of large amounts — shots in quick succession, drinking games, or pre-loading before going out — which can push BAC up faster than the body can show early warning signs. A person can look "asleep" while still absorbing alcohol.

The emergency signs to act on: unresponsive to shaking or shouting, slow or irregular breathing (fewer than about eight breaths per minute or gaps of 10+ seconds between breaths), cold, clammy or bluish skin, vomiting while unconscious, or seizures. If any appear, call local emergency services and keep the person on their side. Do not wait for them to "sleep it off." Our disclaimer explains what this educational site cannot do.

How this connects to the calculators

Questions people ask

What BAC level is considered drunk?

There is no single "drunk" number. In every US state, 0.08% BAC or higher is illegal per se for ordinary adult drivers, but measurable impairment of judgment and reaction begins well below that — often near 0.02–0.05%. Feeling sober is not evidence of being unimpaired.

What is a normal blood alcohol level?

For a person who has not been drinking, BAC is 0.00%. Any value above zero means alcohol is being absorbed or is present. "Normal" does not mean safe to drive once you are above a jurisdiction's legal threshold.

Is a 0.05% BAC illegal?

It depends on the jurisdiction and driver category. Utah sets 0.05% for ordinary drivers; most other US states set 0.08%. Drivers under 21 face zero tolerance, and commercial (CDL) drivers face 0.04%. Many countries set 0.05% or lower.

What BAC is lethal?

Death becomes possible from roughly 0.25–0.30% and increasingly likely above 0.35%, but fatal outcomes have been recorded lower and rare survival higher with emergency care. Lethal levels depend on tolerance, health, food and how fast the alcohol was consumed.

Can I use this chart to check if I am safe to drive?

No. This chart describes population-level, typical effects for education only. Your actual BAC, tolerance, fatigue, medications and health change your impairment. Only a breath or blood test measures BAC, and even a measured value below a legal limit does not prove safe driving.

Does tolerance change these effects?

Experienced drinkers may feel fewer subjective effects at a given BAC, but measurable impairment of reaction, coordination and judgment remains. Tolerance also predicts dependence risk. "Holding your liquor" is not a safety feature.
