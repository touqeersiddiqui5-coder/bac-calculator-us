BAC CALCULATOR

Clear information. Visible assumptions.

We make alcohol calculations easier to understand without turning an estimate into a promise.

What this site is for

BAC Calculator is an independent educational website at bac-calculator-us.com. It combines a browser-based blood alcohol estimator, an alcohol-volume conversion tool and plain-language guides. It is not a medical service, emergency service, law firm or testing laboratory.

Our purpose is to explain the difference between alcohol quantity, estimated concentration, measured concentration and impairment. These are often confused. We keep the calculation assumptions visible and link to public-health and government sources for further reading.

Our editorial approach

Articles begin with a direct answer, then explain context, limitations and related questions. Sources are linked on individual pages. The preparation date describes this website edition; it does not imply that a clinician reviewed the content on that date. We do not claim medical credentials, professional medical review or official endorsement.

Legal examples identify their jurisdiction and should be checked against the linked authority before use. Content may become outdated as guidance or law changes. The methodology page explains the calculation and distinguishes software checks from clinical validation.

How each page is built

Every article on this site follows the same working pattern. We start from the question a reader actually types — “how long does alcohol stay in urine,” “what does 0.08 mean” — and write a direct answer first, then the context that makes the answer honest: what is measured, what varies, and what the number cannot tell you. Claims that carry medical or legal weight are placed next to a link to the relevant public-health or government source, such as NIAAA, CDC, NHTSA or GOV.UK.

Calculators are developed in the opposite direction from content: the math is written down first, tested against worked examples with known inputs, and only then given an interface. The estimation methodology page shows the full equation, the constants and a step-by-step example so any reader can verify the arithmetic by hand. If a calculation cannot be explained in plain language, we do not ship it.

What we deliberately do not do

Sites in this category face recurring temptations, and we have chosen against all of them. We do not display green “you're safe” badges or a legal countdown, because no estimate supports that claim. We do not publish drinking-to-a-target or test-timing guides, because they convert an educational tool into a planning aid for risky behavior. We do not show fake review scores, invented expert bylines or medical-review badges, because borrowed authority is worse than visible humility.

We also do not stuff pages with every keyword variant. Each page answers one question cluster well and links to the neighbouring topic instead of repeating it. That choice costs some search visibility and buys credibility, which matters more in a subject area that touches health and driving law.

Updates and review cadence

The edition date shown on each page records when that page was prepared or revised. Legal figures — drink-drive thresholds, per-se limits, driver-category rules — are re-checked against their official sources whenever the page is revised, and a revision is published if a figure has changed. Health guidance follows the same rule. We cannot guarantee real-time accuracy between revisions, so readers making consequential decisions should always follow the live official source linked on the page.

Significant corrections are made in place rather than silently: the page changes and the edition date moves. If a source page moves or a claim can no longer be supported, the claim is removed rather than kept on a dead link. This is a small, self-funded project; the trade-off for that independence is that updates happen on a best-effort schedule, not on a corporate compliance calendar.

Who the site is written for

The intended reader is an adult who wants to understand how alcohol quantity, concentration, detection and law actually relate — before a night out, the morning after, or when a test result raises questions. The guides are also written to be useful to students, bartenders, HR and safety officers, and anyone supporting a friend or family member. We assume no medical or legal training, which is why each specialist concept is defined where it appears.

People in crisis are not our audience in the sense we can serve: if someone may have alcohol poisoning, the correct page is your local emergency number, not this website. The disclaimer spells out that boundary, and every article repeats the core safety line — estimates are not measurements, and no calculator decides whether it is safe to drive.

Alex Morgan — Alcohol Education & BAC Tools Researcher

I research and build educational tools focused on alcohol pharmacokinetics, blood alcohol concentration (BAC), and real-world drinking safety. I started BAC/clarity out of a simple observation: most people don't think in grams of ethanol or liver elimination curves—they think in pints, rounds, and hours. That disconnect often leads to dangerous assumptions about how sober someone actually is.

My work focuses on demystifying the science behind alcohol metabolism. Rather than treating BAC calculations as a black box, I aim to show the exact arithmetic, the underlying assumptions (like Widmark distribution factors and zero-order elimination rates), and the biological realities—like why food, biological sex, body water, and time make every person’s curve unique. Every guide and formula on this site is cross-referenced with established public-health literature from institutions like the NIAAA, CDC, and NHS.

Just as importantly, I am vocal about what online calculators cannot do. Mathematical estimates can model trends, but they cannot measure stomach emptying, active absorption, or neuromuscular impairment. BAC/clarity is built strictly as an educational learning resource to encourage safer decision-making—it is never a substitute for an evidential breathalyzer, a clinical test, or a green light to get behind the wheel.
